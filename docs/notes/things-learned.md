## 01 &mdash; A wing only makes sense past a certain distance

<p class="learned-meta">Learned during preliminary sizing &middot; September 2026</p>

### What I assumed

That a fixed wing is simply the better way to build a delivery aircraft. Wings generate lift
efficiently, multirotors brute-force their way through the air, so a winged VTOL should beat a
quadcopter at basically anything. Adding a wing felt like an upgrade with no real downside
beyond complexity.

### What is actually true

**A wing has to be paid for before it starts paying out.** The wing, the tilt pivot, the tilt
actuator and the tail are all mass the aircraft carries on every single flight. That mass is
spent whether the mission is 200 metres or 20 kilometres. What the wing gives back is *lower
power in cruise* - and that return only accumulates while the aircraft is actually cruising.

So there is a **break-even distance**. Below it, the wing costs more mass than it saves and a
plain multirotor is genuinely the better aircraft. Above it, the multirotor's battery starts
growing faster than the wing's structure ever did, and the wing wins by an increasing margin.

### Why the multirotor curve bends upward so hard

This is the part I find genuinely interesting. A multirotor has to hover for the entire
mission, so its energy need grows linearly with distance. More energy means a bigger battery.
But a bigger battery is *more mass to hover*, which needs more energy again.

That feedback loop stays gentle for a while and then stops being gentle. Past roughly 8 km in
my case it does not converge at all - the aircraft can no longer lift the battery it would
need. **That is not a limitation of the calculation; it is a real physical wall.** It is the
same reason there is no such thing as a long-range quadcopter.

The winged aircraft has the same feedback loop, but its cruise power is several times lower,
so the loop is far weaker and the curve stays almost flat.

### What it changed about this project

I had originally specified a 500 m mission. At that distance the analysis said the wing was
costing me roughly 0.3 kg for nothing - I would have built a tilt-wing that was objectively
worse than the quadcopter I already know how to build.

**So I changed the mission, not the aircraft.** The design radius is now 4 km, comfortably past
the crossover, which means the wing is justified by what the aircraft has to do rather than by
me wanting to build one. Early flight testing will still happen at short range, because that is
easier and safer - but *testing close in is a test plan, not a design case.*

### The general lesson

A component earns its place by what the mission asks for, not by being more sophisticated.
Before adding something to a design, it is worth asking what the mission would have to look
like for that thing to pay for itself - and then checking whether the actual mission looks
like that.

I would not have found this by reasoning about it. It came out of putting both aircraft
through the same sizing loop and watching the curves cross.
